# Facts used in the film

| Claim | Value used | Source |
|---|---|---|
| The eyes make about three saccades (jumps) per second | 3 / s | Rayner, "Eye movements in reading and information processing", *Psychological Bulletin* 124 (1998): fixations last ~200–300 ms |
| During a saccade, vision is suppressed and the brain removes the blurred input (saccadic suppression / masking) | — | Matin, "Saccadic suppression: a review and an analysis", *Psychological Bulletin* 81 (1974); Ibbotson & Krekelberg, *Current Opinion in Neurobiology* 21 (2011) |
| Added up, more than an hour a day | 3 saccades/s × ~30–50 ms ≈ 9–15 % of viewing time → over 16 waking hours ≈ 1.4–2.4 h; stated conservatively as "über eine Stunde" | computed from the two lines above |
| Mirror test: looking from one of your own eyes to the other in a mirror, you never see them move (someone watching you does) | — | classic demonstration of saccadic suppression (e.g. Ilg & Hoffmann, *Vision Research* 33 (1993); widely used in textbooks) |
| The gap is filled backwards in time: the first second on a clock you just glanced at seems longer ("stopped-clock illusion", chronostasis) | — | Yarrow, Haggard, Heal, Brown & Rothwell, "Illusory perceptions of space and time preserve cross-saccadic perceptual continuity", *Nature* 414 (2001) |

The on-screen 1.6 s hold of the second hand is an illustration of the effect, not a measured value.
