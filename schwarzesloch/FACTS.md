# Facts used in the film

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Near a black hole, gravity pulls harder on the near end of a body than on the far end (tidal force); a falling body is stretched lengthwise and squeezed sideways | standard general relativity / Newtonian tidal force; e.g. NASA Science, "Black Holes" |
| Physicists really call this "spaghettification" | Stephen Hawking, *A Brief History of Time* (1988); the term is in the Oxford English Dictionary; NASA and ESA use it (e.g. ESO press release eso2018, "spaghettification" of a star, 2020) |
| A distant observer sees a falling object slow down as it nears the event horizon; its light is redshifted and dims until it fades from view | gravitational time dilation and redshift; e.g. Kip Thorne, *Black Holes and Time Warps* (1994); NASA Science, "Black Holes" |
| The falling person notices nothing special at the horizon and keeps falling (in their own time it takes a finite time) | standard result of general relativity (proper time to cross the horizon is finite) |

Simplifications: for a stellar-mass black hole spaghettification happens outside the horizon; for a supermassive one it happens only inside. The disk's arch is a stylised picture of gravitational lensing; the slowing clock follows an exponential slowdown chosen for the picture, not a calculated orbit. Colours are kept in the style's warm palette; "röter" is shown as a shift to deep amber.
